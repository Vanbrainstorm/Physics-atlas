The harmonic oscillator: from Newton's law to phase space

Why do springs, pendulums, resonators and even small vibrations of atoms obey essentially the same equation? This chapter derives the common structure rather than asking you to memorize it.

Learning objectives
  • Derive the equation of motion from a force law.
  • Explain why the angular frequency depends on stiffness and inertia.
  • Connect the Newtonian, energy and phase-space descriptions.
  • Identify the domain of validity of the harmonic approximation.
Prerequisites: derivatives, second-order ordinary differential equations, Newton's second law.

1. The physical problem

Imagine a mass attached to a spring on a nearly frictionless horizontal track. Pull the mass a short distance from equilibrium and release it. It returns toward equilibrium, overshoots because it has acquired momentum, and repeats. The central question is not merely “what is the formula?” but “what physical assumptions make this motion sinusoidal?”

Let x be displacement measured from the spring's equilibrium length. For sufficiently small extensions, Hooke's law states that the restoring force is proportional to displacement and points in the opposite direction.

\(F_x=-kx,\qquad k>0\)

The minus sign carries the physics: when the mass is displaced to the right, the force points left; when displaced left, the force points right. The constant k has units N/m and measures stiffness.

2. Modeling assumptions

We initially assume a point mass m, a massless spring, negligible friction, a fixed support, and a linear restoring force. The coordinate is measured from the equilibrium position, so any constant background force has already been absorbed into that position.

Why small oscillations are so universal

Near a stable equilibrium \(x_0\), a smooth potential can be Taylor-expanded: \(V(x_0+\xi)=V(x_0)+V'(x_0)\xi+\frac12V''(x_0)\xi^2+\cdots\). At equilibrium \(V'(x_0)=0\); at a stable minimum \(V''(x_0)>0\). Thus, to lowest nonconstant order, \(V\) is quadratic and \(F=-dV/d\xi\) is linear. The harmonic oscillator is the generic local approximation to stable motion, not just a special property of springs.

3. Derive the motion step by step

Step 1 · Apply Newton's second law.

Acceleration is the second derivative of position. Equating net force to mass times acceleration gives

\(m\ddot{x}=-kx\)

Dividing by mass and defining \(\omega_0^2=k/m\) yields

\(\boxed{\ddot{x}+\omega_0^2x=0},\qquad \omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}\)

Because \(k/m\) has units s\(^{-2}\), \(\omega_0\) has units rad/s (radians are dimensionless). A stiffer spring oscillates faster; a heavier mass oscillates slower.

Step 2 · Solve the differential equation.

Try an exponential \(x=e^{rt}\). Substitution gives \((r^2+\omega_0^2)e^{rt}=0\), so \(r=\pm i\omega_0\). Real solutions can therefore be written

\(x(t)=A\cos(\omega_0t)+B\sin(\omega_0t)\)

Equivalently, \(x(t)=C\cos(\omega_0t+\phi)\), where amplitude \(C\geq0\) and phase \(\phi\) encode initial conditions. The period is \(T=2\pi/\omega_0\).

Step 3 · Impose initial conditions.

For initial position \(x(0)=x_0\) and velocity \(\dot{x}(0)=v_0\), evaluating the solution and its derivative at \(t=0\) gives \(A=x_0\) and \(B=v_0/\omega_0\).

\(\boxed{x(t)=x_0\cos(\omega_0t)+\frac{v_0}{\omega_0}\sin(\omega_0t)}\)

This expression predicts the entire trajectory from just two initial data because the equation is second order.

4. The energy and phase-space viewpoints

Multiply Newton's equation by velocity \(\dot{x}\): \(m\ddot{x}\dot{x}+kx\dot{x}=0\). Both terms are total time derivatives, so

\(\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12m\dot{x}^2+\frac12kx^2\right)=0\)

Hence the total energy \(E=K+V\) is conserved, with kinetic energy \(K=\frac12m\dot{x}^2\) and spring potential \(V=\frac12kx^2\). At a turning point the velocity vanishes and all energy is potential; at equilibrium the potential is zero and speed is greatest.

Define momentum \(p=m\dot{x}\). Then a constant-energy curve satisfies

\(\frac{p^2}{2m}+\frac{kx^2}{2}=E\)

In \((x,p)\) phase space this is an ellipse. The trajectory repeatedly circles that ellipse while its energy stays fixed. In Hamiltonian mechanics the same motion follows from \(H(x,p)=p^2/(2m)+kx^2/2\), with \(\dot{x}=\partial H/\partial p\) and \(\dot{p}=-\partial H/\partial x\).

6. Fully worked example

Problem. A 0.50 kg mass is attached to a spring of stiffness 8.0 N/m. It is released from rest at \(x_0=0.10\) m. Find the angular frequency, period, displacement at \(t=0.20\) s, and total mechanical energy.

Identify known quantities.

\(m=0.50\,\mathrm{kg},\ k=8.0\,\mathrm{N/m},\ x_0=0.10\,\mathrm{m},\ v_0=0\).

Calculate the frequency and period.
\(\omega_0=\sqrt{8.0/0.50}=4.0\,\mathrm{rad/s},\quad T=2\pi/4.0\approx1.57\,\mathrm{s}\)
Apply the initial conditions.

Since the mass is released from rest, the sine term vanishes. At \(t=0.20\) s,

\(x=0.10\cos(4.0\times0.20)\approx0.0697\,\mathrm{m}\)
Calculate the energy and check units.
\(E=\frac12kx_0^2=\frac12(8.0)(0.10)^2=0.040\,\mathrm{J}\)

The units are \((\mathrm{N/m})\mathrm{m}^2=\mathrm{N\,m}=\mathrm{J}\). Energy remains constant in the ideal model.
